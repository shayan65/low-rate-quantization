\begin{abstract}
We report a controlled study of post-training weight quantization below two bits
in a hybrid linear-attention language model, asking which factors actually move
validation loss under measured storage budgets: codebook dimension, error
compensation, index rate, and which tensors are converted at all. On the
DeltaNet QKV projections of a 0.8B model, a shared eight-dimensional codebook
reduces damage relative to a learned three-level scalar code by
$0.036118$ NLL (95\% CI $[0.033768, 0.038414]$) at $0.43\%$ more payload bytes,
and the direction reproduces on the 48 corresponding projections of a 27B
checkpoint over its complete validation stream ($0.007121$, CI
$[0.005725, 0.008513]$, $0.019\%$ more bytes). Two
factors dominate that codec choice. GPTQ-style error compensation removes
$68$--$69\%$ of the damage on both tensor families, roughly three times what the
codec contributes, and it does so while \emph{raising} plain weight MSE, which
inverts the proxy the earlier literature and our own earlier drafts relied on.
Index rate dominates in turn: over $1.6$ to $3.2$ bits, damage falls
approximately exponentially, halving about every $0.48$ bits, with no knee
visible in the measured interval. Coverage matters more than any of them --- MLP
weights are $2.79\times$ more damaging per parameter than the projections --- and
a Hessian-derived per-tensor ranking intended to exploit that fact performs
\emph{worse than random selection} of the same byte budget, the second
forward-side layer-ranking failure we record. We state boundaries explicitly:
these are partial-coverage, short-context quality and payload-storage results on
two checkpoints and one corpus, and they do not establish full-model quality or
runtime feasibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Scope and contributions}

Vector quantization with Hessian-aware compensation, and Hadamard incoherence
processing with eight-dimensional codes, are established
ideas~\cite{gptvq,quipsharp,gptq,quip}. We do not claim a new algorithm; the
block extension of GPTQ in Section~\ref{sec:method} is a routine generalization
and we treat it as implementation, not contribution. What we offer is controlled
evidence, with explicit boundaries, about a setting the literature covers
thinly: rates below two bits, on the recurrent projections of a hybrid model,
with storage measured from bytes that are actually written and weights that are
actually read back.

Concretely:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Dimension.} A shared 6561-point eight-dimensional codebook beats a
learned three-level scalar code at near-equal payload bytes, on two tensor
families of a 0.8B model and on the QKV projections of a 27B model
(Table~\ref{tab:primary}). Eight dimensions earn their place: on the frozen
27B run they beat four by $0.004034$ (CI $[0.002715, 0.005334]$), more than half
of the eight-dimensional code's entire advantage over scalar. A 512-block pilot
had suggested the opposite, with four dimensions capturing $95.4\%$ of the gain;
that estimate did not survive the full stream.
\item \textbf{Compensation dominates the codec.} Error compensation is worth
$68$--$69\%$ of the damage against the codec's $6$--$32\%$, and it improves loss
while degrading weight MSE.
\item \textbf{Rate dominates in turn}, falling exponentially with no knee over
the measured interval (Table~\ref{tab:rate}).
\item \textbf{Coverage dominates all of them}, and the obvious way to exploit
that --- ranking tensors by activation-weighted error per byte --- is worse than
random.
\item \textbf{Two negative results on proxies and one on method.} Weight MSE and
activation-weighted error each predict loss inside the regime where they are
fitted and fail outside it; a self-consistent calibration loop is unnecessary
because the dependency graph is acyclic.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What this paper does not establish}

We separate these from the results because earlier drafts of this work did not,
and the reader should not have to reconstruct the boundary.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No full-model conversion.} Every measurement covers a subset:
$15.1\%$ of parameters (0.8B QKV), $35.1\%$ (0.8B MLP), $50.2\%$ (both), or
$9.36\%$ (27B QKV). No end-to-end quantized checkpoint was produced or
evaluated.
\item \textbf{No runtime claim.} We report payload bytes and complete artifact
file bytes. Packed weights fitting in VRAM does not establish that execution
fits: KV cache, recurrent state, activations, kernel workspaces and decoding
buffers are unmeasured. There is no packed inference kernel.
\item \textbf{No four-bit deployment claim.} A historical $+0.008393$ NLL
four-bit measurement exists, but it is a 0.8B projection-subset result
($0.24\%$ of baseline NLL, $0.84\%$ relative perplexity --- distinct
quantities). It is not a 27B result and not a full-coverage result. An earlier
draft used it as both; that claim is withdrawn.
\item \textbf{No causal scaling statement.} The two checkpoints differ in
generation, coverage, tensor dimensions, calibration size and evaluated text
extent. That the 27B margins are smaller is an observation, not an explained
consequence of scale.
\item \textbf{No equivalence results.} Where an interval includes zero we say
\emph{inconclusive}. We prespecified no equivalence margin, and failing to
reject zero is not evidence of no effect.
\end{itemize}

\section{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

All arms share one protocol, and the properties below are the ones earlier
versions of this work got wrong.

\textbf{Storage is measured, not derived.} Every codec serializes to packed
bytes --- five trits per byte, or $c$ base-$K$ codes per \texttt{uint64} with
$K^{c} < 2^{64}$ --- and bits per weight is computed from \texttt{len(bytes)},
not from $\log_2 K / d$. Multi-symbol packing makes the ternary and
vector formats cost the same per index to within a few bytes per tensor, which
is the conservative choice as well as the realistic one.

\textbf{The evaluated weights are the stored weights.} Group scales are rounded
to FP16 \emph{before} quantization, and every tensor is decoded back from its
serialized bytes and required to match the quantizer's reconstruction exactly
before it is installed. In the confirmation run the artifact is additionally
written to disk, reloaded and re-decoded. Omitting this check cost an earlier
draft six points of headline: FP16 scale storage costs a 6561-point codebook
$+0.019$ NLL while leaving a 3-point code unchanged.

\textbf{Byte matching is near-equal, not literal.} Where we say byte-matched,
dimension-4 costs $+0.0029\%$ over scalar at 0.8B and $+0.00012\%$ at 27B;
dimension-8 costs $+0.43\%$ and $+0.0193\%$. Tables report five decimals.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Token-weighted NLL over the complete validation stream
including the ragged final block, with a paired block bootstrap over
$\ge 5000$ resamples. Calibration uses the training split. Table~\ref{tab:coverage}
gives coverage and calibration size per run; they are not identical across runs,
which is why cross-run differences are not attributed to scale.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Rotation is a blockwise randomized Hadamard transform on the input dimension.
Codebooks are fitted by Lloyd/$k$-means in group-normalized space and shared
across all target tensors, charged once.

Extending GPTQ to a codec that quantizes $d$ columns jointly is the only
non-standard step. For a block of columns $J$ the cost charged by the GPTQ
derivation and the compensation applied to the remainder are
\[
\|(W_J-Q_J)\,U_{JJ}^{-1}\|_F^2,
\qquad
W_{\text{rest}} \mathrel{-}= (W_J-Q_J)\,U_{JJ}^{-1}U_{J,\text{rest}},
\]
with $U$ the upper Cholesky factor of $H^{-1}$. The within-block decision is
therefore a metric nearest-neighbour search with $M = U_{JJ}^{-1}$: transform
points and codebook by $M$ and search normally. At $d=1$ this must reduce to
textbook scalar GPTQ, and our test suite verifies it does so bit-exactly against
an independently written reference. We regard this as a routine generalization.

\subsection{Dimension helps, and eight dimensions earn their place}

At near-equal payload bytes the eight-dimensional code beats the learned scalar
ternary code on both 0.8B tensor families and on the 27B projections
(Table~\ref{tab:primary}). Against a scalar control that spends $6.4\%$
\emph{more} bytes the margin is $-0.034303$ $[-0.036660,-0.031971]$ at 0.8B
under the matched compensated recipe; at 27B, over the complete validation
stream, it is $-0.007836$ $[-0.009247,-0.006356]$ at a payload ratio of
$0.93261$ --- the vector code wins while storing $6.7\%$ \emph{fewer} bytes. A
512-block pilot of the same comparison was inconclusive
($-0.001736$ $[-0.004444,+0.000923]$).

On the frozen confirmation, dimension-8 beats dimension-4 by $-0.004034$
$[-0.005334,-0.002715]$ --- more than half of dimension-8's entire advantage
over the scalar code. The 512-block pilot had dimension-4 capturing $95.4\%$ of
the gain, with dimension-8 ahead by only $0.000181$; that reading did not
survive the complete stream. The difference is not sampling: the pilot's blocks
and the remaining $1{,}530$ agree with each other, and BF16 loss on the pilot's
blocks is identical across runs to all printed digits. What differs is
calibration size ($32{,}768$ against $65{,}536$ tokens), which moved the
storage-dominance contrast across non-overlapping intervals on identical
evaluation data. Every interval reported here is conditional on one fitted
quantizer, and calibration variability is not inside it.

\subsection{Compensation dominates the codec, and inverts the proxy}

Error compensation removes $68.3\%$ of the damage on the 0.8B projections and
$69.0\%$ on the MLPs --- both far larger than the codec effect. It achieves this
while \emph{raising} plain weight MSE by $38\%$
($7.18\to9.89\times10^{-5}$), so across recipes weight MSE is anti-predictive
even though within a fixed recipe its ordering matches the loss ordering
exactly. Diagonal activation weighting, by contrast, is worthless: harmful on
scalar codes ($+0.004485$), and a tie once compensation is present
($+0.000206$), because the block metric already carries the diagonal and the
cross terms.

\subsection{Rate dominates compensation}

Over $1.600$ to $3.200$ index bits at fixed $50.2\%$ coverage, damage halves
every $0.451$, $0.507$, $0.416$ and $0.530$ bits --- approximately exponential
across a $1.93\times$ range of payload bytes, with no knee visible
(Table~\ref{tab:rate}). Five rates on one checkpoint at one coverage support
``approximately exponential over this interval'' and nothing stronger; a
matched four-bit point on these same tensors is missing and would be the single
most informative addition.

\subsection{Coverage dominates rate, and cannot be exploited by ranking}

MLP weights are $2.79\times$ more damaging per parameter than the projections.
Given a fixed extra-byte budget, confining it to the MLP family beats spending
it on the projections by $-0.061105$ $[-0.064430,-0.057804]$.

The natural refinement --- rank all 90 tensors by activation-weighted error
removed per extra byte --- \emph{fails}. It promotes 14 of 18 projections and
none of the 48 \texttt{up}/\texttt{down} projections, and loses to random
selection of the same budget by $+0.061244$ $[+0.058083,+0.064344]$. Per
parameter the projections carry $5.7\times$ more activation-weighted error while
causing $2.79\times$ less loss damage, so across families the proxy is
anti-correlated with its target. The quantity measures error injected at a
layer's output and carries no information about propagation to the loss. This is
the second forward-side ranking failure we record; a Fisher-based diagnostic
gave Spearman $+0.015$.

\section{Negative results retained from earlier work}

Two hypotheses were pursued at length and refuted; they are retained because
their failure mode recurs.

\textbf{Polar weight codes.} Storing a magnitude and a phase index per weight
pair appeared to beat an equal-byte real four-bit code. It beat only a control
with a \emph{uniform} codebook; giving the scalar control the same Lloyd--Max
fitting reverses the ranking. Repairing the polar assignment changes MSE by
$0.035\%$, because with five phase bits $1-\cos(\pi/32) \le 4.8\times10^{-3}$
while one radius-grid step is $\approx 14\%$ of the row scale.

\textbf{Self-consistent calibration.} Solving activation-aware calibration as a
DFT-style fixed point is unnecessary: the forward-only dependency graph is
acyclic, so one ordered pass is exact, and closed-form one-pass corrections
already exist~\cite{gptaq}. Measured, sequential capture is worth $-0.001890$
$[-0.003421,-0.000315]$ at $3.5\times$ the cost.

\textbf{The pattern.} Both, and the ranking failure above, are the same error:
a method beat a control that had been given less tuning, or a proxy was trusted
outside the regime where it was fitted. Every correction in this paper came from
a control --- a fitted scalar baseline, a random allocation, a bit-exactness
assertion --- and not from inspection.

\section{Reproducibility and limitations}

Runs record a manifest: arguments, source hashes, checkpoint hashes, library and
CUDA versions, device map, GPU budget, calibration size, and the evaluated
versus available target counts. Tables in this paper are generated directly from
saved results by \texttt{experiments/make\_tables\_v2.py}; an earlier draft
mixed an uncompensated margin into a compensated comparison, and generation is
how we prevent recurrence rather than by care.

\textbf{An exactness failure, observed once and not reproduced.} One run at a
17\,GiB GPU weight budget raised the per-tensor bit-exactness check with a
decoded-versus-reference difference of $0.036$. Seven subsequent runs at that
budget, across two script versions in fresh processes, instead terminated with
an identical out-of-memory error before reaching that state; 15 and 16\,GiB are
exact. The corruption was never reproduced and no failing tensor was captured,
so it could not be localized. One observation of a wrong value stands against
seven of a clean failure, and an earlier draft's framing of this as a
characterized budget-sensitive defect is withdrawn. Every reported run used
15\,GiB, every tensor passed the strict check, and the confirmation run
additionally re-decodes each artifact from disk.

\textbf{Statistical scope.} Intervals measure text-sampling variability
conditional on one fitted quantizer; they do not include calibration or
codebook-seed variability. Adjacent 128-target blocks may be dependent even with
state reset, so block-bootstrap intervals may be optimistic. Validation text has
guided many decisions in this project, so the confirmation run freezes the
recipe and prespecifies its contrasts.
