\begin{center}\small
Controlled post-training quantization study on one 0.8B and one 27B hybrid
linear-attention checkpoint. No quantization-aware training, no serving stack,
and no claim of a new algorithm.
\end{center}

\begin{abstract}
We report a controlled study of post-training weight quantization below two bits
in a hybrid linear-attention language model, asking which factors move validation
loss under storage measured from bytes actually written. Three findings are worth
more than the codec comparisons that motivated the study. First, \textbf{byte
allocation dominates codec design at this scale}: quantizing the tied embedding
costs $0.000098$ NLL per 100\,MB saved against $0.020873$ for the first step of
the weight codec and $2.127315$ for its last, so a model with 2.792-bit weights
and an INT4 embedding is half the size and takes a fifth of the damage of one
with ternary weights and a BF16 embedding. Every sub-two-bit configuration we
measure is off the efficient frontier for whole-model size --- a statement about
parameter shares, since the embedding is $33.8\%$ of this checkpoint but
$9.15\%$ of the 27B one. Second, \textbf{the calibration distribution is a lever
that costs no bytes}: damage is minimized when calibration and evaluation domains
match, and a prespecified $50/50$ mixture at the same token budget cuts
worst-domain damage below either pure condition over three disjoint draws
($0.095211$ against $0.147013$ and $0.191296$), with a half-budget control
showing this is coverage of activation directions rather than sample count.
Third, \textbf{the codec results hold but are smaller than the above}: a shared
eight-dimensional codebook beats a learned three-level scalar code by $0.036118$
NLL $[0.033768, 0.038414]$ at $0.43\%$ more payload bytes on 0.8B DeltaNet
projections and keeps its sign under eight refits; GPTQ-style compensation
removes $68$--$69\%$ of the damage, roughly three times the codec's contribution,
while \emph{raising} plain weight MSE; index rate then dominates compensation and
coverage dominates rate, yet a Hessian-derived per-tensor ranking built to
exploit coverage is worse than random selection. The direction reproduces on a
27B checkpoint, but eight refits there span $-0.007049$ to $+0.000185$ and the
effect is smaller than the spread refitting induces ($0.46\times$ the refit range
against $5.05\times$ at 0.8B), so we report it as a direction on average and lean
on it nowhere. Finally, the converted model is \textbf{served on compressed
weights}: all 186 non-embedding matrices replaced by code-and-codebook modules
reproduce the decoded-BF16 loss to $1.6\times10^{-5}$ at $1.92\times$ less
resident memory by the allocator, and $3.0\times$ cuBLAS latency on an isolated
projection once the required Hadamard rotation is fused. We claim no new
algorithm, and we separate what is established from what is not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Scope and contributions}

Vector quantization with Hessian-aware compensation, and Hadamard incoherence
processing with eight-dimensional codes, are established
ideas~\cite{gptvq,quipsharp,gptq,quip}. We do not claim a new algorithm; the
block extension of GPTQ in Section~\ref{sec:method} is a routine generalization
and we treat it as implementation, not contribution. What we offer is controlled
evidence, with explicit boundaries, about a setting the literature covers
thinly: rates below two bits, on the recurrent projections of a hybrid model,
with storage measured from bytes that are actually written and weights that are
actually read back.

Concretely, and in order of how much they move a deployment decision rather
than of how much work they took:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Byte allocation dominates codec design at this scale}
(Section~\ref{sec:coverage-runtime}). Crossing the weight-rate ladder with four
embedding precisions, the marginal cost of a saved byte differs by four orders
of magnitude between the two axes, and a configuration with higher-rate weights
and a coarser embedding dominates every sub-two-bit configuration in both size
and loss. The embedding quantizer we use is deliberately naive, which makes the
comparison conservative against the codec. The conclusion follows from parameter
shares and therefore inverts with scale, which we state quantitatively rather
than leaving as a caveat.
\item \textbf{Calibration is the lever that costs nothing}
(Section~\ref{sec:calibration}). Damage is minimized when calibration and
evaluation domains match, and a prespecified $50/50$ mixture at the same token
budget reduces worst-domain damage below either pure condition on three disjoint
draws; a half-budget control separates domain coverage from sample count. Unlike
dimension, rate and coverage, this is paid for in neither bytes nor calibration
tokens. We give the identity that explains why mismatch has an effect and why
weight MSE cannot see it.
\item \textbf{The converted model is served on compressed weights}
(Section~\ref{sec:coverage-runtime}), reproducing its decoded-BF16 loss to
$1.6\times10^{-5}$ at a resident footprint measured from the allocator, which is
below what payload arithmetic predicts because the served index layout is wider
than the stored one.
\item \textbf{Dimension.} A shared 6561-point eight-dimensional codebook beats a
learned three-level scalar code at near-equal payload bytes, on two tensor
families of a 0.8B model and on the QKV projections of a 27B model
(Table~\ref{tab:primary}). Eight dimensions earn their place at 0.8B, where the
margin over four keeps its sign in all eight refits ($0.005101$ to $0.018548$).
At 27B the frozen run gives $0.004034$ (CI $[0.002715, 0.005334]$), but that is
a single refit and we did not measure this contrast across refits there; given
that the larger VQ8-against-scalar contrast varies six-fold over three 27B
refits, the 27B dimensionality margin should be treated as unreplicated. A
512-block pilot had suggested the opposite ordering, with four dimensions
capturing $95.4\%$ of the gain; that estimate did not survive the full stream.
\item \textbf{Compensation dominates the codec.} Error compensation is worth
$68$--$69\%$ of the damage against the codec's $6$--$32\%$, and it improves loss
while degrading weight MSE.
\item \textbf{Rate dominates in turn}, falling exponentially with no knee over
the measured interval (Table~\ref{tab:rate}).
\item \textbf{Coverage dominates all of them}, and the obvious way to exploit
that --- ranking tensors by activation-weighted error per byte --- is worse than
random.
\item \textbf{Two negative results on proxies and one on method.} Weight MSE and
activation-weighted error each predict loss inside the regime where they are
fitted and fail outside it; a self-consistent calibration loop is unnecessary
because the dependency graph is acyclic.
\item \textbf{Robustness of the main result.} On these 0.8B DeltaNet
projections the eight-dimensional code's advantage survives refitting over
calibration draws and seeds, held-out text, a second domain, and context
extension through 2048 tokens. Its magnitude varies across those conditions,
and finer scalar grouping shows no consistent benefit in any of them.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work and what is new here}

Every technique this paper uses is established. Hessian-weighted error
compensation is GPTQ~\cite{gptq} and its asymmetric refinement~\cite{gptaq};
incoherence processing by random Hadamard rotation is QuIP~\cite{quip} and
QuIP\#~\cite{quipsharp}, and QuaRot~\cite{quarot} applies the same idea to
activations for 4-bit inference; multi-dimensional codebooks are
GPTVQ~\cite{gptvq} and, in additive form, AQLM~\cite{aqlm}. Our block extension
of GPTQ to vector codes (Section~\ref{sec:method}) is a routine generalization
that collapses to textbook scalar GPTQ at dimension one, which we verify
bit-exactly; we treat it as implementation.

What we contribute is controlled evidence in a setting the literature covers
thinly, and three results that the literature's framing tends to hide.

\textbf{The architecture.} Published low-rate results concentrate on dense
attention transformers. The recurrent projections of a hybrid linear-attention
model have a different error-propagation structure --- a quantized
\texttt{in\_proj\_qkv} perturbs a recurrent state rather than a one-shot
attention map --- and we find effects there that a transformer study would not
surface, including damage that depends on position within a block.

\textbf{Calibration sensitivity is usually a robustness check; here it is the
headline.} Williams and Aletras~\cite{calibdata} show that calibration choice
materially changes post-training quantization and pruning outcomes. We take that
further in one specific direction: because the calibration set costs no stored
bytes, its effect is not a confound to be controlled but a free parameter to be
optimized, and we show a prespecified fixed-budget mixture beats every pure
condition on worst-domain damage.

\textbf{Byte accounting for the whole model, not the covered tensors.} Low-rate
papers report bits per weight over the tensors they convert. That number is the
right one for comparing codecs and the wrong one for sizing a model. Measuring
the whole checkpoint --- and then measuring the served model from the
allocator --- is what surfaces the result that at this scale the embedding, not
the codec, governs the byte budget.

\section{What this paper does not establish}

We separate these from the results because earlier drafts of this work did not,
and the reader should not have to reconstruct the boundary.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No general claim that embeddings should be quantized before
weights.} Our byte-allocation result is measured on one 0.8B checkpoint, with
one naive embedding quantizer, on one corpus. It follows from the embedding's
$33.8\%$ parameter share, and we show that share is $9.15\%$ on the 27B
checkpoint, where the same arithmetic points the other way --- but we did not
measure embedding quantization at 27B, so the inversion is an argument about
bytes, not a result. Nor did we try rotation or error compensation on the
embedding, so its ladder is a floor rather than a characterization.
\item \textbf{No serving stack.} A full-coverage conversion is served on
compressed weights and measured from the allocator
(\S\ref{sec:coverage-runtime}), but the embedding, norms, KV cache and
recurrent state stay BF16, it runs on one GPU at batch one, and both kernels are
tuned only far enough to be correct.
\item \textbf{No latency claim.} A compressed matmul is verified and measured
(\S\ref{sec:coverage-runtime}), and it is $3.0\times$ \emph{slower} than a BF16
cuBLAS product at batch one. The memory reduction is real; a speed advantage is
not established, and neither kernel is tuned beyond correctness.
\item \textbf{No four-bit deployment claim.} A historical $+0.008393$ NLL
four-bit measurement exists, but it is a 0.8B projection-subset result
($0.24\%$ of baseline NLL, $0.84\%$ relative perplexity --- distinct
quantities). It is not a 27B result and not a full-coverage result. An earlier
draft used it as both; that claim is withdrawn.
\item \textbf{No causal scaling statement.} The two checkpoints differ in
generation, coverage, tensor dimensions, calibration size and evaluated text
extent. That the 27B margins are smaller is an observation, not an explained
consequence of scale.
\item \textbf{No equivalence results.} Where an interval includes zero we say
\emph{inconclusive}. We prespecified no equivalence margin, and failing to
reject zero is not evidence of no effect.
\end{itemize}

\section{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

All arms share one protocol, and the properties below are the ones earlier
versions of this work got wrong.

\textbf{Storage is measured, not derived.} Every codec serializes to packed
bytes --- five trits per byte, or $c$ base-$K$ codes per \texttt{uint64} with
$K^{c} < 2^{64}$ --- and bits per weight is computed from \texttt{len(bytes)},
not from $\log_2 K / d$. Multi-symbol packing makes the ternary and
vector formats cost the same per index to within a few bytes per tensor, which
is the conservative choice as well as the realistic one.

\textbf{The evaluated weights are the stored weights.} Group scales are rounded
to FP16 \emph{before} quantization, and every tensor is decoded back from its
serialized bytes and required to match the quantizer's reconstruction exactly
before it is installed. In the confirmation run the artifact is additionally
written to disk, reloaded and re-decoded. Omitting this check cost an earlier
draft six points of headline: FP16 scale storage costs a 6561-point codebook
$+0.019$ NLL while leaving a 3-point code unchanged.

\textbf{Byte matching is near-equal, not literal.} Where we say byte-matched,
dimension-4 costs $+0.0029\%$ over scalar at 0.8B and $+0.00012\%$ at 27B;
dimension-8 costs $+0.43\%$ and $+0.0193\%$. Tables report five decimals.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Token-weighted NLL over the complete validation stream
including the ragged final block, with a paired block bootstrap over
$\ge 5000$ resamples. Calibration uses the training split. Table~\ref{tab:coverage}
gives coverage and calibration size per run; they are not identical across runs,
which is why cross-run differences are not attributed to scale.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Figure~\ref{fig:method} shows the pipeline and the two layouts it produces.

Rotation is a blockwise randomized Hadamard transform on the input dimension.
Codebooks are fitted by Lloyd/$k$-means in group-normalized space and shared
across all target tensors, charged once.

Extending GPTQ to a codec that quantizes $d$ columns jointly is the only
non-standard step. For a block of columns $J$ the cost charged by the GPTQ
derivation and the compensation applied to the remainder are
\[
\|(W_J-Q_J)\,U_{JJ}^{-1}\|_F^2,
\qquad
W_{\text{rest}} \mathrel{-}= (W_J-Q_J)\,U_{JJ}^{-1}U_{J,\text{rest}},
\]
with $U$ the upper Cholesky factor of $H^{-1}$. The within-block decision is
therefore a metric nearest-neighbour search with $M = U_{JJ}^{-1}$: transform
points and codebook by $M$ and search normally. At $d=1$ this must reduce to
textbook scalar GPTQ, and our test suite verifies it does so bit-exactly against
an independently written reference. We regard this as a routine generalization.

\subsection{Dimension helps, and eight dimensions earn their place}

At near-equal payload bytes the eight-dimensional code beats the learned scalar
ternary code on both 0.8B tensor families and on the 27B projections
(Table~\ref{tab:primary}; the full arm-by-arm ablations are
Tables~\ref{tab:v4}, \ref{tab:mlp} and \ref{tab:q27}). Against a scalar control that spends $6.4\%$
\emph{more} bytes the margin is $-0.034303$ $[-0.036660,-0.031971]$ at 0.8B
under the matched compensated recipe; at 27B, over the complete validation
stream, it is $-0.007836$ $[-0.009247,-0.006356]$ at a payload ratio of
$0.93261$ --- the vector code wins while storing $6.7\%$ \emph{fewer} bytes. A
512-block pilot of the same comparison was inconclusive
($-0.001736$ $[-0.004444,+0.000923]$).

On the frozen confirmation, dimension-8 beats dimension-4 by $-0.004034$ with
an interval of $[-0.005334,-0.002715]$ --- more than half of dimension-8's entire advantage
over the scalar code, though on a single refit; at 0.8B the same contrast keeps
its sign across all eight refits. The 512-block pilot had dimension-4 capturing
$95.4\%$ of the gain, with dimension-8 ahead by only $0.000181$; that reading
did not survive the complete stream. The difference is not sampling. The pilot's blocks
and the remaining $1{,}530$ agree with each other, and BF16 loss on the
pilot's blocks is identical across runs to all printed digits. What differs
is calibration size, $32{,}768$ tokens against $65{,}536$, which moved the
storage-dominance contrast across disjoint intervals on the same evaluation
data. Every interval reported here is conditional on one fitted
quantizer, and calibration variability is not inside it.

\subsection{Compensation dominates the codec, and inverts the proxy}

Error compensation removes $68.3\%$ of the damage on the 0.8B projections and
$69.0\%$ on the MLPs --- both far larger than the codec effect. It achieves this
while \emph{raising} plain weight MSE by $38\%$
($7.18\to9.89\times10^{-5}$), so across recipes weight MSE is anti-predictive
even though within a fixed recipe its ordering matches the loss ordering
exactly. Diagonal activation weighting, by contrast, is worthless: harmful on
scalar codes ($+0.004485$), and a tie once compensation is present
($+0.000206$), because the block metric already carries the diagonal and the
cross terms.

\subsection{Rate dominates compensation}

Over $1.600$ to $4.000$ index bits at fixed $50.2\%$ coverage, damage halves
every $0.451$, $0.506$, $0.416$, $0.530$ and $0.468$ bits --- approximately
exponential across a $2.40\times$ range of payload bytes, with no knee visible
(Table~\ref{tab:rate}). Six rates on one checkpoint at one coverage support
``approximately exponential over this interval'' and nothing stronger.

The four-bit point is measured on these same tensors: $+0.018572$ NLL,
$+1.87\%$ perplexity. It is \emph{not} interchangeable with the historical
four-bit figure of $+0.008393$, which covers 15.1\% of parameters rather than
$50.2\%$.

Figure~\ref{fig:rate} plots the curve; the exponential fit and the absence
of a knee are easier to see than to tabulate.

\subsection{Coverage dominates rate, and cannot be exploited by ranking}

MLP weights are $2.79\times$ more damaging per parameter than the projections.
Given a fixed extra-byte budget, confining it to the MLP family beats spending
it on the projections by $-0.061105$ $[-0.064430,-0.057804]$.

The natural refinement --- rank all 90 tensors by activation-weighted error
removed per extra byte --- \emph{fails}. It promotes 14 of 18 projections and
none of the 48 \texttt{up}/\texttt{down} projections, and loses to random
selection of the same budget by $+0.061244$ $[+0.058083,+0.064344]$. Per
parameter the projections carry $5.7\times$ more activation-weighted error while
causing $2.79\times$ less loss damage, so across families the proxy is
anti-correlated with its target. The quantity measures error injected at a
layer's output and carries no information about propagation to the loss. This is
the second forward-side ranking failure we record; a Fisher-based diagnostic
gave Spearman $+0.015$.

\subsection{How far the main result travels}

Two studies probe the conditions under which the eight-dimensional advantage
was measured, one varying the fit and the other the evaluation.

\textbf{Refitting} (Table~\ref{tab:refit}). Eight refits over three disjoint
calibration draws, three codebook seeds and four calibration sizes. All three
primary contrasts keep their sign in $8/8$ refits; VQ8 against scalar ranges
from $-0.037809$ to $-0.031061$. The ranges over refits are of the same order
as, and in several cases larger than, the text-bootstrap half-width, so
magnitudes are quoted as ranges rather than single margins. VQ8's advantage
grows monotonically with calibration size ($-0.033335$ at 16k tokens to
$-0.037809$ at 131k), which is a condition effect rather than noise.

\textbf{At 27B} the ordering transfers on both axes --- a second corpus
($-0.008768$) and 2048-token context ($-0.005542$), both excluding zero --- but
eight refits show the effect is smaller than the variability that refitting
induces. The refit range is $0.007234$ at 27B against $0.006748$ at 0.8B,
essentially the same absolute spread, while the mean effect is
$-0.003345$ against $-0.034095$. Seven of eight refits favour the vector code,
four are individually significant, and one favours scalar. Refit variability
does not shrink with scale; the effect does, so the 27B result is reported as
a direction on average and the paper does not lean on it.

\textbf{Varying the evaluation} (Table~\ref{tab:gen}). One fitted quantizer
evaluated on the never-used wikitext-2 test split, on TinyStories, and at
$512$ and $2048$ token contexts. All three primary contrasts exclude zero in
every configuration. Through 2048 tokens there is no ordering reversal and no
large increase in aggregate damage, though VQ8's advantage does shrink about
$18\%$ from the 128-token setting; aggregate loss averages over positions, so
this is not evidence against recurrent-state drift. Out of domain, VQ8's
absolute advantage grows ($0.038806 \to 0.043775$) while the fraction of scalar
damage it removes falls ($32.0\%\to24.0\%$); we do not attribute the higher
out-of-domain damage to calibration mismatch, which would need two calibration
domains crossed with two evaluation domains.

\textbf{The scalar group-size control} has no stable sign: it flips across
refits and, on TinyStories, excludes zero in the \emph{unfavourable} direction
($+0.010942$). Section~\ref{sec:calibration} resolves this into two opposed
effects of similar size.

\section{Calibration mismatch: an identity, a bound, and a crossed test}
\label{sec:calibration}

Changing the calibration corpus changes damage substantially while changing
storage not at all, which makes it the sharpest lever in this study. The
mechanism is visible in the objective the compensation step actually minimizes.

\subsection{Why mismatch has an effect at all}

Write $E = \widehat{W} - W$ for the weight error a codec leaves behind. For a
fixed input distribution $D$ the layer reconstruction objective is quadratic,
\begin{equation}
L_D(E) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_D \lVert E x \rVert_2^2
      \;=\; \operatorname{tr}\!\left( E H_D E^{\top} \right),
\qquad H_D = \mathbb{E}_D\!\left[ x x^{\top} \right],
\end{equation}
so evaluating on one domain a quantizer fitted on another costs exactly
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{test}}(E) - L_{\mathrm{cal}}(E)
  \;=\; \operatorname{tr}\!\left[ E \left( H_{\mathrm{test}} - H_{\mathrm{cal}} \right) E^{\top} \right],
\label{eq:mismatch}
\end{equation}
and this is bounded elementarily by
\begin{equation}
\left\lvert L_{\mathrm{test}}(E) - L_{\mathrm{cal}}(E) \right\rvert
  \;\le\; \lVert H_{\mathrm{test}} - H_{\mathrm{cal}} \rVert_2 \, \lVert E \rVert_F^2 .
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}

\emph{This is an identity and an elementary bound, not a theorem and not a
statement about NLL.} It says only that the penalty depends on how the error
$E$ is \emph{oriented} relative to the difference of second moments: a pattern
that is cheap under one domain's activations can be expensive under another's,
and two quantizers with identical $\lVert E \rVert_F$ can differ arbitrarily
in \eqref{eq:mismatch}. Plain weight MSE is exactly $\lVert E \rVert_F^2$ up
to a constant and therefore cannot see the difference at all --- which is the
same blindness that makes it invert against loss in our compensation and
group-size results. Hessian-weighted reconstruction objectives of this form
underpin GPTQ~\cite{gptq} and GPTVQ~\cite{gptvq}.

\subsection{A fixed-budget calibration mixture}

Equation~\eqref{eq:mismatch} suggests an intervention. For the quadratic
objective, $H_{\mathrm{mix}} = \tfrac12 H_{\mathrm{WT}} + \tfrac12
H_{\mathrm{TS}}$ represents \emph{exactly} the equally weighted average of the
two domains' reconstruction losses. Whether that improves worst-domain NLL does
not follow --- the objective is a local proxy and the mixture halves the data
seen from each domain --- so we measure it. The split is prespecified at 50/50
rather than tuned, the calibration budget is held at the same 65,536 tokens as
every other condition, and each domain's calibration text is disjoint from the
text either is evaluated on. Because the crossed cells were inspected before
this condition was designed, we report it as an \emph{exploratory
intervention}.

Over three disjoint calibration draws (Table~\ref{tab:mixture}) the mixture's
worst-domain damage is lower than either pure condition's, for both codes, at
identical storage: mean $0.095211$ for VQ8 against $0.147013$ and $0.191296$, a
$35\%$ reduction against the better pure condition, and $0.143482$ for scalar
against $0.190325$ and $0.282648$ ($25\%$). The price falls on the matched
domain and is small --- against the half-budget wikitext condition the mixture
gives up $0.007311$ on wikitext and gains $0.113104$ on TinyStories, a
sixteen-to-one trade, and all sixteen mixed-versus-pure contrasts keep their
sign across all three draws.

\textbf{This is domain coverage, not sample count.} The mixture averages two
$32{,}768$-token Hessian estimates while each pure condition uses one
$65{,}536$-token estimate, so we ran the control: halving the wikitext budget
costs $+0.008921$, while spending that same halved budget on a second domain
instead gains $-0.060724$. Coverage is worth about seven times what sample
count is worth at this budget, and in the opposite direction. This is
consistent with the mixture leaving a direction unprotected only when both
domains do, though we inspected no directions and do not claim the mechanism is
demonstrated.

Judged by this paper's own yardstick, the gain is $4.2\times$ the mixture's
refit range for VQ8 and $16.8\times$ for scalar --- the range of the 0.8B codec
result ($5.05\times$), not the 27B one ($0.46\times$). This remains one model,
one recipe, two corpora and one split; it does not establish that mixing is
optimal or that $50/50$ is the right weighting.

\subsection{The group-size control, read as a factorial}

The unstable group-size contrast has the same character. Moving from g128 to
g64 changes the grouping \emph{and} refits the shared codebook in the new
normalized space, so the end-to-end contrast ($+0.000737$,
$[-0.001213,+0.002710]$) is a diagonal of a $2\times2$, not a main effect. An
earlier draft read it as ``group size alone moves nothing''; that reading is
withdrawn. Read as the factorial it is (Table~\ref{tab:g64}), all four
single-factor contrasts exclude zero and only the diagonal does not: moving to
g64 \emph{hurts} by $+0.014019$ or $+0.006568$ depending on which alphabet is
held fixed, and adopting the finer-fitted codebook helps by $-0.005831$ or
$-0.013281$ depending on which grouping is held. Main effects are $+0.010293$
(grouping) and $-0.009556$ (codebook) with a $+0.007451$ interaction: two
opposed effects of similar size, whose near-cancellation is what makes the
end-to-end contrast small.

contrast by exactly zero. Repeating the $2\times2$ over six refits (three
disjoint draws, three calibration sizes), three of the four single-factor
contrasts change sign themselves, and the one that varies most (grouping at a
fixed g64 codebook, range $0.015179$) varies more than the diagonal it was meant
to explain (range $0.010206$); the interaction excludes zero in none of the six.
The cancellation account of the \emph{instability} is therefore withdrawn. What
the design does establish, since the codebooks are provably fixed, is that every
effect in this family is a function of the compensation statistics alone, and
that those statistics --- estimated from 16k--131k calibration tokens --- are
noisy enough to move each contrast by $0.005$ to $0.015$, comparable to or
larger than the contrasts themselves. We also note that the single-fit factorial
above was evaluated on wikitext-2 test while the sign instability it was invoked
to explain was measured on validation; the refit study is on validation and
reproduces that study's diagonal exactly.

That replacement account was itself given falsifiable predictions and tested
against a reference Hessian from $524{,}288$ tokens with four disjoint draws at
each of four calibration sizes. Two hold: the Hessian's relative deviation falls
with calibration tokens at a log-log slope of $-0.468$ against the $-0.5$ of
finite-sample noise, and the contrast's within-size standard deviation falls at
$-0.494$ --- two exponents that have no reason to agree unless calibration
sampling is what moves the contrast. The third fails: within a size, the
correlation between a draw's Hessian deviation and its contrast deviation is
$-0.142$ over 16 draws, i.e.\ none. Equation~\eqref{eq:mismatch} explains why we
should not have expected otherwise --- the penalty depends on how the Hessian
error is \emph{oriented} relative to the weight error, and a Frobenius norm is
orientation-blind. We therefore claim only what was tested: the spread scales
like finite-sample noise, and no scalar measure of Hessian error predicts an
individual refit. One further observation was not predicted by anything: all
four draws at $65{,}536$ tokens give a negative contrast and all four at
$131{,}072$ a positive one, so calibration size shifts this quantity's sign
rather than merely adding noise to it.

Damage across disjoint tensor families is \emph{approximately} additive at this

to full coverage is largely composition, though we cannot attribute all of it to
the added $120{,}127{,}488$ parameters, since the full-coverage run also refits
its codebook over a four-times-larger pool.

The byte accounting is the more consequential result. The covered matrices
compress $9.28\times$ ($995 \to 107$ MB), but the model shrinks only
$2.44\times$: the embedding, which \texttt{tie\_word\_embeddings} also makes the
output head, stays at BF16 and is then $83\%$ of what remains, putting the
effective rate at $6.559$ bits/weight while the covered tensors sit at $1.725$.
\textbf{At these rates the binding constraint is embedding precision, not the
weight codec} (Figure~\ref{fig:bytes}), and crossing the two axes shows the exchange rate is not close
(Table~\ref{tab:embed}). The embedding quantizer is deliberately naive --- per-row
or per-group scaling, no rotation, no compensation --- and still, in $\Delta$NLL
per 100\,MB saved, moving it from BF16 to INT8 costs $0.000098$ against
$0.020873$ for the first step of the weight codec and $2.127315$ for its last.
The conjecture that FP8 on the embedding would save more bytes than moving the
covered tensors from $2.792$ to $1.725$ bits, at far less cost, holds with room
to spare: $253.8$\,MB against $66.4$\,MB, at $+0.001434$ against $+0.828532$.

The consequence is a dominance rather than a trade-off
(Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}). A model with 2.792-bit
weights and an INT4 embedding is $305.8$\,MB at $+0.208926$; one with ternary
weights and a BF16 embedding is $616.9$\,MB at $+1.000022$ --- half the size and
a fifth of the damage. \emph{On this model, every sub-two-bit configuration we
measured is off the efficient frontier for whole-model size.} This does not
weaken the codec comparisons, which hold coverage and bytes fixed; it withdraws
the implicit premise that driving the weight rate down is how to make this model
smaller. It is also a statement about parameter shares rather than about
codecs: the embedding is $33.8\%$ of this checkpoint on a $248{,}320$-token
vocabulary, while on the 27B checkpoint --- same vocabulary, hidden size $5120$,
untied head --- embedding and head together are $2{,}542{,}796{,}800$ of
$27{,}781{,}427{,}952$ parameters, $9.15\%$ counted from the checkpoint index,
and the arithmetic inverts. Nothing here measures the embedding's quality cost
at 27B, so that inversion is an argument about bytes and not a result.

\subsection{A compressed matmul on a real projection}

Storage layout is not runtime layout: five base-$6561$ codes in a \texttt{uint64}
give an optimal $1.600$ bits of index but are inconvenient to index, since
$6561^5 > 2^{63}$ sets the sign bit. PyTorch will hold a \texttt{uint64} tensor
but raises \texttt{NotImplementedError} on its arithmetic, so the host cannot do
the digit extraction; Triton does have \texttt{tl.uint64}, and there the
extraction costs five integer divisions per word on the critical path. One code
per \texttt{int16} costs $2.000$ bits instead. Nor
is the runtime activation the stored one: the codes hold $\mathrm{rotate}(W)$,
so a compressed path must apply the same blockwise Hadamard to its input,
$y = \mathrm{rotate}(x)\,\widehat{W}^{\top}$.

On \texttt{in\_proj\_qkv} of the 0.8B model quantized with the full recipe, and
on activations captured from the model, the Triton path's relative error
against an exact FP32 product is $2.71\times10^{-7}$, against
$3.55\times10^{-3}$ for the installed BF16 weights that every $\Delta$NLL in
this paper was measured with (all paths scored on the same 256 rows with the
same denominator). \emph{The compressed kernel closely reproduces the quantized
FP32 reference on the tested activations; its end-to-end quality relative to
decoded BF16 execution remains unmeasured.} An earlier draft called the reported
NLL figures ``conservative'' on this evidence; that does not follow, since BF16
rounding can reinforce or partially cancel quantization error and the nonlinear
layers downstream need not respond monotonically to a local perturbation.

Resident bytes fall $7.08\times$ on that tensor and $7.47\times$ at the 27B QKV
shape, the difference being codebook amortization; peak allocation during a
product falls $5.00\times$ ($113.5 \to 22.7$ MB). Latency does not improve, though it is far better than our first
implementation suggested. With the rotation written as ten Python-controlled
butterfly stages the compressed path took $0.5233$ ms against $0.0187$ ms for
cuBLAS, $0.4760$ ms of it in the transform. That measured the prototype, not the
transform: the radix-2 FWHT in natural order is a Kronecker product, so a
1024-point block viewed as a $32\times32$ tile $X$ satisfies
$\mathrm{FWHT}(x) = \mathrm{vec}(H_{32} X H_{32})/32$ --- two small matrix
products in one kernel with the sign flip folded in. Fused, the rotation costs
$0.0182$ ms ($26.1\times$ faster, bit-identical to the reference on this input)
and the whole compressed path $0.0555$ ms, or $\mathbf{3.0\times}$ cuBLAS rather
than $28\times$. That agrees with the $3.1\times$ GEMV-only comparison at the
larger synthetic shape, where no transform is in the loop. The format is
verified, smaller, and about three times slower at batch one; neither kernel is
tuned beyond correctness.

\subsection{A model that runs on compressed weights}

Every quality figure above was produced by decoding packed bytes into BF16
weights and installing those. Replacing all 186 non-embedding matrices with
modules that hold only int16 codes, an FP16 codebook and FP16 group scales ---
dense weights dropped, rotation fused --- gives a model that runs
(Table~\ref{tab:e2e}). It reproduces the decoded-BF16 loss to
$-1.59\times10^{-5}$, well inside the evaluation's own noise, so the served
model is the model this paper measured. Its resident footprint, taken from the
allocator rather than from a payload count, is $783.2$\,MB against $1504.8$, a
factor of $1.92$ --- \emph{below} the $2.20\times$ the byte arithmetic gives at
this rate, because the served layout spends 2.000 bits of index where the file
spends 1.600. A single decode step costs $99.4$\,ms against $85.6$\,ms. Both
figures are dominated by an unfused recurrent path --- the installed library
reports that the fast linear-attention kernel is unavailable, and $85.6$\,ms per
token for a 0.8B model is not a serving number --- so the $1.16\times$ ratio is
the increment compressed weights add to an unoptimized loop and nothing more. We
do not report a decode-throughput claim; the interpretable latency measurement
is the isolated projection above.

\section{Negative results retained from earlier work}

Two hypotheses were pursued at length and refuted; they are retained because
their failure mode recurs.

\textbf{Polar weight codes.} Storing a magnitude and a phase index per weight
pair appeared to beat an equal-byte real four-bit code. It beat only a control
with a \emph{uniform} codebook; giving the scalar control the same Lloyd--Max
fitting reverses the ranking. Repairing the polar assignment changes MSE by
$0.035\%$, because with five phase bits $1-\cos(\pi/32) \le 4.8\times10^{-3}$
while one radius-grid step is $\approx 14\%$ of the row scale.

\textbf{Self-consistent calibration.} Solving activation-aware calibration as a
DFT-style fixed point is unnecessary: the forward-only dependency graph is
acyclic, so one ordered pass is exact, and closed-form one-pass corrections
already exist~\cite{gptaq}. Measured, sequential capture is worth $-0.001890$
$[-0.003421,-0.000315]$ at $3.5\times$ the cost.

\textbf{The pattern.} Both, and the ranking failure above, are the same error:
a method beat a control that had been given less tuning, or a proxy was trusted
outside the regime where it was fitted. Every correction in this paper came from
a control --- a fitted scalar baseline, a random allocation, a bit-exactness
assertion --- and not from inspection.

\section{Reproducibility and limitations}

Runs record a manifest: arguments, source hashes, checkpoint hashes, library and
CUDA versions, device map, GPU budget, calibration size, and the evaluated
versus available target counts. Tables in this paper are generated directly from
saved results by \texttt{experiments/make\_tables\_v2.py}; an earlier draft
mixed an uncompensated margin into a compensated comparison, and generation is
how we prevent recurrence rather than by care.

\textbf{An exactness failure, observed once and not reproduced.} One run at a
17\,GiB GPU weight budget raised the per-tensor bit-exactness check with a
decoded-versus-reference difference of $0.036$. Seven subsequent runs at that
budget, across two script versions in fresh processes, instead terminated with
an identical out-of-memory error before reaching that state; 15 and 16\,GiB are
exact. The corruption was never reproduced and no failing tensor was captured,
so it could not be localized. One observation of a wrong value stands against
seven of a clean failure, and an earlier draft's framing of this as a
characterized budget-sensitive defect is withdrawn. Every reported run used
15\,GiB, every tensor passed the strict check, and the confirmation run
additionally re-decodes each artifact from disk.

\textbf{Statistical scope.} Intervals measure text-sampling variability
conditional on one fitted quantizer, and that conditioning is load-bearing.
Refitting over three calibration draws and three codebook seeds produces
ranges of the same order as, and in several cases larger than, the
text-bootstrap half-width; calibration size is reported separately again, as a
condition change rather than a further sample. We report the two statistics
side by side and do not divide them: a range over a handful of refits and a
bootstrap half-width are different quantities, and their ratio has no coverage
interpretation. Directions survive --- the vector code wins in all eight refits
on all three primary contrasts --- but a single six-decimal margin from one
refit is more precise than the procedure warrants, so magnitudes are quoted as
ranges ($-0.031$ to $-0.038$ for VQ8 against scalar). The scalar group-size
contrast changes sign across refits and across evaluation domains, and the
factorial in Section~\ref{sec:calibration} shows why: grouping and codebook are
opposed effects of similar size, so their end-to-end difference is small and
unstable. The 27B intervals carry the same conditioning; refit variability
\emph{is} now measured there (Section~\ref{sec:results}) and is larger than the
effect.

\textbf{Generalization scope.} The five evaluation configurations in
Section~\ref{sec:results} are two corpora and three context lengths, not five
independent replications; the three wikitext-test rows share their text.
Position-resolved loss is measured with per-block intervals over 127 blocks,
and we report it as weak evidence and lean on it nowhere. An earlier draft said
VQ8 damage ``rises monotonically'' across a 2048-token block; with intervals
the endpoint contrast does not exclude zero and the rise is not monotone. What
erodes is VQ8's \emph{advantage} over the scalar code, $+0.009695$ from the
first bucket to the last, and that excludes zero on all five bootstrap seeds at
$100{,}000$ replicates --- but by a lower bound between $5\times10^{-5}$ and
$1.3\times10^{-4}$, two orders of magnitude below the effect, and the analysis
followed inspection of the bucket means rather than preceding it. Scalar damage
shows no trend. Position and token content are confounded within a block in any
case, so we report position-dependent damage and do not identify
recurrent-state drift as its mechanism; that would need the same tokens
evaluated with and without a state reset, which was not run. Higher damage out of domain is attributed to
calibration mismatch on the strength of the crossed design in
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}, which rules out the alternative reading that
TinyStories is intrinsically harder for this recipe; it does not decompose the
mismatched figure into mismatch and sensitivity components.
